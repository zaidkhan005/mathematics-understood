## Version 1.1 change record

Version 1.1 makes three requirements explicit and mandatory across the
series: (1) readers must learn to construct mathematical models and
equations, not only interpret supplied formulas; (2) readers must be
trained to investigate conjectures, construct and critique proofs, and
solve unfamiliar problems; and (3) mathematically relevant diagrams,
graphs, plots, geometric figures, tables, and other visual explanations
are part of the teaching system, not optional decoration. These changes
are integrated into the principles, teaching frameworks, chapter
architecture, assessment standards, pilot gates, and release checklist.

## Version 1.2 change record

Version 1.2 adds an explicit goal of developing readers' visualization
and representation-building abilities, not merely supplying finished
pictures. Visual scaffolds will be deliberately faded when pedagogically
appropriate: readers predict, annotate, complete, sketch, reconstruct,
transform, and translate between representations with increasing
independence. Short, mandatory-in-the-flow prompts belong beside the
explanation at the moment they are useful, not solely in end-of-chapter
exercise lists. This policy also recognizes that learners differ in
mental imagery; vivid internal pictures are not a prerequisite, and
external sketching, structured descriptions, and accessible alternatives
remain valid routes to visual reasoning.

## Version 1.3 change record

Version 1.3 makes a problem-first opening the default for conceptual
lessons where learners have enough prior knowledge to make a meaningful
attempt. The sequence is: state a specific problem or challenge; invite a
brief attempt, prediction, or conjecture; develop intuition from the
problem and the learner's existing ideas; connect representations; teach
the precise formal structure; compare the explanation with plausible
attempts; then practise, transfer, and retrieve. The initial attempt is
not a test of worth and need not succeed. Staged hints, a clear reveal,
and an explanation that addresses common approaches are required.

This rule is evidence-informed, not universal. Research on problem-solving
before instruction reports average benefits for conceptual learning and
transfer, with effects depending on implementation, learner/task
characteristics, and outcomes. The design therefore includes explicit
exceptions and retains worked examples or minimal orientation where an
unsupported initial search would be unproductive. This project has not
yet demonstrated that its own version of the method improves learning;
pilot testing remains necessary.

# 1. Mission and promise

## 1.1 Mission

Create a rigorous, welcoming, self-contained mathematics education
series that helps learners build usable intuition, precise formal
knowledge, procedural fluency, mathematical reasoning, and the ability
to apply ideas independently—regardless of their educational background.

The project must teach more than how to reproduce a procedure. Learners
should increasingly understand what mathematical objects represent, why
relationships have the structure they do, what assumptions a method
requires, how a result follows, and where that result can be used.

## 1.2 Intended learner outcome

After studying a concept, a learner should be able to do as many of the
following as are appropriate to that concept:

- Explain the idea in their own words without relying on a memorized
  phrase.

- Interpret its symbols, quantities, relationships, assumptions, and
  units where relevant.

- Move between useful verbal, numerical, graphical, geometric, and
  symbolic representations, including constructing one representation
  from another.

- Construct equations, inequalities, functions, diagrams, graphs, or
  other mathematical models from a description or unfamiliar situation,
  stating necessary assumptions and checking the result.

- Reconstruct or justify the central rule or derivation instead of
  merely recalling it.

- Form plausible conjectures, test examples and edge cases, identify
  counterexamples to false claims, and construct valid arguments or
  proofs at the level appropriate to the topic.

- Calculate accurately and select an appropriate method without being
  prompted with a formula.

- Identify boundary conditions, counterexamples, exceptions, or
  limitations.

- Apply the concept to a changed or unfamiliar problem without being
  told which method to use.

- Interpret, create, annotate, and reconstruct useful visual
  representations; reason about how they change; and detect when a graph,
  diagram, or visual analogy is incomplete or misleading.

- Build increasing independence in visual reasoning. This does not
  require everyone to experience vivid mental imagery: sketching,
  structured verbal/spatial reasoning, and accessible alternatives are
  legitimate tools.

- Connect it to earlier mathematics and, where relevant, to a scientific
  model.

Not every objective applies identically to every topic. Abstract proof,
for example, requires forms of reasoning that cannot always be grounded
in a physical analogy. The promise is a supported path to rigor, not the
elimination of all difficulty.

The project does not claim that mathematics is literally a natural
language, that every formula has a simple everyday translation, or that
a single teaching method has already been proven superior across all
ages and branches of mathematics. Those are claims to investigate, not
assumptions to advertise.

## 1.4 How success will be judged

Success is not page count, visual polish, learner praise alone, or the
number of examples generated. The principal indicators are:

- Mathematical and scientific correctness.

- Clear and faithful explanations.

- Independent usability.

- Accurate execution and justification.

- Transfer to unfamiliar problems.

- Delayed retention where it can be assessed.

- Consistency and traceability across the full collection.

This balanced view is informed by the National Research Council’s
account of mathematical proficiency, which treats conceptual
understanding, procedural fluency, strategic competence, adaptive
reasoning, and productive disposition as connected strands rather than
independent goals \[1\]. The report is a useful framework, not proof
that this exact project design will succeed; our method must be tested.

# 2. Audience, scope, and boundaries

## 2.1 Primary audience

The intended audience is broad: complete beginners, school learners,
adult returners, university students, independent learners, and advanced
readers who want a clearer conceptual account of topics they already
use.

The curriculum must not assume one uniform route through every chapter.
It will provide:

- Explicit prerequisites and diagnostic checks.

- Multiple entry points where prior knowledge differs.

- Short repair sections for common prerequisite gaps.

- Clear links to prerequisite chapters.

- Advanced extensions without making them mandatory for every reader.

## 2.2 Scope of the core series

The core mathematics collection is intended to progress from basic
quantities and arithmetic through algebra, geometry, functions,
trigonometry, logic, calculus, linear algebra, probability and
statistics, differential equations, and selected advanced topics such as
real analysis, abstract algebra, complex analysis, topology, and measure
theory.

The detailed list, order, volume boundaries, and depth are not frozen in
v1.3. They will be established by a prerequisite and concept-dependency
map before full book production. Some topics require parallel pathways
rather than one linear route.

## 2.3 Scope of the science companion series

A connected application collection will show how mathematical ideas are
used in physics, chemistry, biology, and broader scientific modeling.
Applications will be tied to core prerequisites and will distinguish
among:

- Mathematical identities or theorems.

- Assumptions of a physical or scientific model.

- Empirical laws and approximations.

- Numerical or symbolic calculations.

- Interpretations that are helpful pedagogically but are not themselves
  mathematical proofs.

The science books will not be used to smuggle unexplained mathematics
into the core curriculum. When required mathematics is introduced in an
application chapter, the chapter must either teach the required idea
adequately or point to a suitable core treatment and provide a short,
correct refresher.

## 2.4 Out of scope for the initial phase

- Draft the entire collection in one pass.

- Promise a fixed number of volumes or publication date.

- Claim measured learning benefits without comparative evidence.

- Treat an AI-generated answer or citation as verified merely because it
  sounds plausible.

- Require learners to use paid software or a specific device to complete
  the core books.

- Make visual effects, gamification, or analogies more important than
  mathematical truth.

# 3. Non-negotiable principles

## 3.1 Problem first; intuition before formalism, where appropriate

For a suitable conceptual lesson, begin with a specific, mathematically
meaningful problem, question, prediction, or claim before giving the main
intuitive explanation. Invite the reader to try a small first attempt or
state a conjecture using what they already know. The attempt may be
partial or incorrect; it exists to activate prior knowledge and make the
learning need visible, not to punish gaps in knowledge.

Then build intuition around the problem and the learner's plausible
approaches. Connect intuitive meaning to diagrams, graphs, examples, and
symbolic representations before or alongside formal definitions. Give
precise instruction, derivation, or proof, and explicitly compare it with
the first attempt so the reader can see what was useful, incomplete, or
mistaken. Intuition still precedes formalism; the new default is that a
problem opens the door to intuition.

Do not force problem-first exploration onto every topic. When prerequisites
are missing, the search space is too large, the target is completely
unfamiliar, or a procedural worked example is the more responsible entry
point, provide a short orientation or a worked example first. The author
must choose deliberately and record why. Never abandon a self-study
reader in unproductive struggle: supply graduated hints, a clear route to
the explanation, and feedback after the attempt.

## 3.2 Rigor without compromise

Intuition supplements formal mathematics; it does not replace
definitions, valid derivation, proof, exact conditions, or correct
calculation. Every claim must be stated at the level of precision
appropriate to the chapter.

## 3.3 Multiple representations with explicit connections

Use verbal descriptions, number lines, diagrams, tables, graphs,
physical models, symbolic expressions, and code when each genuinely
helps. Visual representations are part of the explanation whenever the
concept depends on spatial, graphical, structural, geometric, or data
relationships. Do not decorate the page with a representation that adds
no understanding. Explain how representations connect and where they
cease to be equivalent. When useful, ask learners both to interpret a
visual and to construct one themselves.

## 3.4 Every operation is interpreted in context

The books must explain why operations appear in particular expressions.
They must not force one simplistic translation onto a symbol.
Multiplication may express scaling, area, a product of quantities,
composition, or a different structure; division may express a ratio,
rate, inverse operation, or normalization. The correct interpretation
depends on definitions and context.

## 3.5 Derivations explain decisions

Worked derivations should tell learners why a relation is introduced,
why a transformation is allowed, what assumptions are being used, and
how the result can be checked. Algebraic steps should not be skipped
simply because an experienced author finds them obvious.

## 3.6 Understanding must transfer

Learners need tasks that change the numbers, representation,
assumptions, or context. Familiar-example imitation is not sufficient
evidence of mastery.

## 3.7 Independent learning is a design constraint

Each chapter must include enough orientation, explanation, practice,
feedback, and solution support for a learner to study alone. Do not rely
on a teacher being present to fill in missing logic.

## 3.8 Accessibility without dilution

Use plain language where it is accurate. Define unavoidable technical
vocabulary. Avoid unnecessary jargon, but introduce the correct term
when learners need it. “Easy to read” must never mean “less precise.”

## 3.9 Scientific honesty

State the scope and limits of analogies and models. Distinguish exact
results from approximations, empirical observations, conjectures, and
interpretations. Do not imply a causal or physical explanation where the
mathematics alone does not establish one.

## 3.10 Learnability over spectacle

Every figure, sidebar, analogy, story, interaction, or visual element
must support understanding, memory, reasoning, or application.
Engagement is valuable only when it serves learning.

## 3.11 Visual mathematics is core infrastructure

The series will not default to text-only exposition when a visual would
materially support understanding. Depending on the topic, teaching
assets may include number lines, arrays, fraction and area models,
coordinate plots, function graphs, geometric constructions, annotated
proof diagrams, transformations, vectors, flowcharts, tables, statistical
plots, histograms, probability trees, vector fields, 2D cross-sections,
3D schematics, and carefully chosen scientific diagrams. The asset must
serve a specific learning outcome; not every chapter needs every type.

A chapter about graphs must include actual, accurate graphs and guided
work in reading, interpreting, sketching, or constructing them. A chapter
about geometry must use diagrams where they support spatial reasoning.
A diagram-dependent proof must not rely on a figure alone when formal
justification is needed. If a visual is central to the idea, removing it
should make the explanation materially harder to understand; otherwise
its purpose should be reconsidered.

Every mathematical visual must be checked against the mathematics.
Axes, scales, tick marks, units, labels, domains, ranges, orientations,
legend keys, and assumptions must be shown when relevant. Graphs must
not misrepresent the function, data, or relationship; geometry drawings
must distinguish schematic appearance from stated mathematical facts.
Color must not be the only way to encode meaning. Captions and nearby
text should explain what to notice, and the visual must remain legible at
its intended print or screen size. Alt text or an equivalent text
description should be supplied for meaningful figures.

Prefer original, purpose-built visuals that match the teaching sequence.
Record provenance and permissions for any reused asset. A visually
polished but mathematically false figure is a correctness defect, not a
design issue. Final review must inspect every page, equation, graph,
diagram, table, and caption.

## 3.12 Build visualization ability; do not create visual dependence

The goal is not for readers to rely forever on diagrams supplied by the
author. The goal is to help them choose, make, interpret, and adapt
representations when a problem calls for them. A supplied visual is a
teaching scaffold and a valid mathematical tool—not a substitute for the
reader's own reasoning.

When appropriate, use a gradual progression: (1) study a clear example
and learn what its parts mean; (2) annotate or explain it; (3) predict a
missing feature or complete a partly finished visual; (4) construct a
visual from words, data, an equation, or stated conditions; (5)
reconstruct or redraw it without copying the displayed solution; and
(6) transfer the skill to changed conditions or a new context. Not every
lesson needs all six steps, and the sequence may loop back when learners
need support.

Place brief prompts directly in the body of the explanation at the
moment they matter. Examples include “Pause: before looking below, sketch
what you expect,” “Predict the graph's shape before we plot it,” “Draw
your own diagram from these conditions,” “What changes if this parameter
increases?”, or “Cover the diagram and reconstruct its key relationships
from memory.” These are instructional checkpoints, not optional
end-of-chapter extras. They should be visually recognizable but should
not interrupt the logic of the explanation.

Reveal the reference visual or worked construction after the learner
has had a reasonable chance to try. Then compare the learner's version
with the reference and explain meaningful differences. Avoid treating
one stylistic drawing as the only acceptable result when several
representations are mathematically sound. For proofs, diagrams may help
readers discover a route, but the proof must separately justify the
claim. For graphs, readers must learn to sketch and reason from equations,
tables, and contexts—not only admire a supplied plot.

Do not confuse visualization training with requiring vivid mental
pictures. Some learners do not form voluntary mental imagery easily.
Use accessible alternatives such as drawing, stepwise verbal or spatial
descriptions, tactile or screen-reader-compatible representations where
feasible, and structured symbolic reasoning. The objective is flexible
representational reasoning, not a test of a person's subjective imagery.

# 4. The Intuition-to-Formalism Bridge (IFB)

The working teaching method is named the Intuition-to-Formalism Bridge
(IFB). It is an editorial framework to be tested and refined, not a
claim of a newly validated theory of learning.

## 4.1 Problem-first, intuition-to-formalism sequence

The default sequence for suitable conceptual lessons is:

> **1. Problem statement:** Pose a concrete question, problem, pattern,
> prediction, or claim before explaining the target concept. State exactly
> what the reader is being asked to find, compare, construct, explain, or
> decide.
>
> **2. First attempt:** Invite the reader to try briefly, predict an
> outcome, draw a rough sketch, generate one or more approaches, or state
> a conjecture using prior knowledge. Do not imply they must already know
> the formal method. Make pausing and attempting worthwhile, with a hint
> available when needed.
>
> **3. Intuition:** Build an initial mental model around the problem.
> Use the reader's plausible ideas as a bridge to the concept, and show
> why the problem calls for a more precise mathematical tool.
>
> **4. Representations:** Connect verbal, numerical, graphical,
> geometric, tabular, and symbolic views where useful. Ask for a
> prediction, sketch, annotation, or partial construction before
> revealing a finished visual when this serves the learning goal.
>
> **5. Precise language:** Introduce the quantities, definitions,
> notation, assumptions, and technical terms needed to describe the
> relationships accurately.
>
> **6. Formal structure:** State the mathematical relation, theorem,
> procedure, definition, model, or proof target precisely.
>
> **7. Explanation and justification:** Derive the result or prove the
> claim with assumptions and permitted steps explicit. Explain why the
> method works, not merely what steps to copy.
>
> **8. Compare and consolidate:** Return to the initial problem and
> attempt. Identify which ideas were useful, why incomplete approaches
> failed or needed refinement, and how the formal method resolves the
> issue. Treat errors as information, not as a personal failure.
>
> **9. Guided practice:** Work through carefully chosen examples while
> explaining decisions, checks, and alternative representations.
>
> **10. Independent practice:** Progress from execution to explanation,
> construction, proof, strategy selection, and transfer to unfamiliar
> problems.
>
> **11. Boundaries and connections:** Examine exceptions, counterexamples,
> limiting cases, alternative methods, and links to other topics.
>
> **12. Retrieval and return:** Revisit the idea later through cumulative
> questions and spaced review, including at least some prompts without
> worked steps immediately available.

This is a design default, not a rigid script. A problem-first opening
should make later instruction more meaningful by activating existing
ideas and exposing the question the new concept answers. It should be
carefully chosen so that a learner can make a useful attempt even if they
cannot solve it. The subsequent explanation must connect explicitly to
that attempt; simply placing a hard question at the beginning and then
ignoring it does not satisfy this standard.

A meta-analysis of 53 studies with 166 comparisons reported a moderate
average advantage for problem-solving followed by instruction over
instruction followed by problem-solving (Hedges' g = 0.36), with stronger
effects when the design closely followed productive-failure principles
[7]. A randomized mathematics study also reported benefits for
conceptual understanding and transfer in its particular setting [8].
These findings support considering the sequence; they do not prove that
it is best for every age, topic, learner, or implementation. The review
reported moderator patterns, including contrasts for younger learners
and domain-general skills [7]. The project must use pilot testing and
retain explicit exceptions rather than claim universal superiority.

## 4.2 The operation-interpretation protocol

- What are the objects and quantities?

- What relationship is claimed?

- Why is the selected operation appropriate in this context?

- Which definitions, laws, assumptions, or modeling choices justify it?

- What does the expression imply when one quantity changes?

- What are the units, domain restrictions, sign behavior, and limiting
  cases, where applicable?

- Is there a counterexample to an oversimplified verbal interpretation?

## 4.3 Illustrative example: electrostatic interaction energy

**U(r) = k q₁q₂ / r, k = 1 / (4π ε₀)**

For two point charges in the electrostatic setting, with zero potential
energy chosen at infinite separation, the interaction energy is given by
the expression above.

A sound intuitive treatment should derive the structure rather than
assign arbitrary meanings to the symbols. The potential created by the
first point charge is proportional to q₁/r; the potential energy of the
second charge in that potential is q₂ times the potential. This leads to
the product q₁q₂ and the inverse distance dependence. The sign depends
on the charge signs and the chosen reference convention. The model and
its assumptions must be stated.

For the dipole example, the learner then sums the four pairwise charge
interactions with their appropriate signs and separations. If a
distant-dipole approximation is introduced, the chapter must explain the
geometry, approximation conditions, and orientation dependence. It must
not suggest that the simplified pair-charge expression alone
automatically gives every dipole interaction result.

chapter.*

## 4.4 The Concept-to-Equation Method (CEM)

CEM is the standard scaffold for learning to formulate mathematics. It
complements IFB; it is not a replacement for topic-specific reasoning.

1. **Describe the situation.** State what is happening, what is being
   asked, and what the model needs to explain.
2. **Identify the quantities.** Separate known values, unknowns,
   variables, parameters, constants, and relevant objects.
3. **Choose relationships.** Ask which quantities depend on, compare
   with, constrain, or change with other quantities.
4. **Choose representations.** Use words, diagrams, tables, graphs,
   arithmetic, symbols, or combinations as useful.
5. **Construct the mathematics.** Form an equation, inequality,
   function, system, sequence, geometric relation, or other suitable
   object.
6. **State constraints.** Specify assumptions, domain, units, signs,
   initial or boundary conditions, and approximation limits where
   relevant.
7. **Validate.** Test cases, check dimensions or units where
   applicable, examine extreme or boundary cases, and interpret the
   result in the original context.
8. **Revise if needed.** A model that fails its checks must be corrected
   or its limitations stated.

The workflow is iterative, not a rigid recipe. Some situations do not
contain enough information to determine a unique model; learners must
recognize this and state what additional assumptions or data would be
needed. Exercises should move from guided formulation to independent
construction in unfamiliar settings.

## 4.5 The Mathematical Discovery-to-Proof Method (MDP)

MDP develops the ability to move from a question or observed pattern to
a justified mathematical claim. It is a teaching scaffold, not a
universal algorithm for discovery.

1. **Notice or ask.** Identify a pattern, puzzle, relationship, or
   claim worth investigating.
2. **Explore examples.** Calculate cases, draw figures, use tables or
   computation, and compare representations to understand the behavior.
3. **State a conjecture.** Write the suspected claim precisely, including
   the domain and quantifiers.
4. **Try to break it.** Check small cases, boundary conditions, special
   cases, and plausible counterexamples. Examples can refute a universal
   claim but finitely many examples do not generally prove it.
5. **Choose a strategy.** Consider direct proof, algebraic
   transformation, induction, contrapositive, contradiction, construction,
   a lemma, a known theorem, or another method appropriate to the claim.
6. **Build the argument.** State definitions and assumptions, justify
   every important inference, and make the logical structure visible.
7. **Audit the proof.** Check that the argument proves the exact claim,
   handles all required cases, and does not assume what it seeks to prove.
8. **Extend the result.** Where appropriate, consider alternative
   proofs, stronger or weaker claims, generalizations, and conditions
   under which the statement fails.

Learners must practise both reading finished proofs and producing their
own. Proof instruction should begin with age- and topic-appropriate
justification in foundational chapters, then become increasingly
explicit and formal as the curriculum develops. A proof is judged by
logical validity, not by whether the discovery process was elegant.

## 4.6 Three directions of mathematical learning

Chapters and cumulative assessments should deliberately develop three
directions, in proportions appropriate to the topic:

- **Intuition to mathematics:** construct an expression, equation,
  graph, model, conjecture, or proof from a situation or idea.
- **Mathematics to intuition:** interpret what an equation, graph,
  theorem, transformation, or abstract object says and what it implies.
- **Mathematics to new mathematics:** derive consequences, transform
  expressions validly, prove related claims, and connect structures.

Mastery is incomplete when a learner can only follow one direction.

# 5. Chapter and exercise standards

## 5.1 Standard chapter architecture

- Prerequisites: What the reader should already know; short diagnostic
  questions.

- Learning outcomes: Observable capabilities, not vague statements such
  as “understand the topic.”

- Problem statement / challenge (before the main explanation): State a
  specific question, problem, prediction, construction, or claim that
  naturally calls for the target concept. It should be mathematically
  meaningful, accessible enough to attempt, and precise about the task.

- First attempt before instruction: Invite a brief try, prediction,
  rough sketch, conjecture, or one or more possible strategies. State or
  imply that a complete solution is not required. Provide staged hints or
  a skip-to-explanation route so independent learners are not stranded.

- Motivating question / purpose: Explain why the problem matters and what
  broader idea it opens; do not replace the concrete challenge with a
  vague story.

- Intuitive entry point (developed after the challenge): Build the mental
  model around the problem, the reader's prior knowledge, and the
  tentative approaches they may have tried.

- Representation bridge: Connections among words, pictures, numerical
  examples, graphs, and symbols. Include a purposeful visual plan: which
  diagrams, graphs, plots, or tables are essential, what each teaches,
  and how the learner will interpret or construct it.

- Embedded think-and-do checkpoints: Short prompts placed inside the
  explanation, beside the relevant idea or figure. Ask readers to predict,
  sketch, annotate, complete, reconstruct, or transform a representation
  before the explanation supplies the whole answer. Do not relegate all
  visualization practice to the final exercise section.

- Mathematical construction: At least one formulation task where
  appropriate: the learner constructs an equation, model, graph,
  diagram, or other representation instead of only using one supplied.

- Discovery and proof: Include appropriate tasks for conjecturing,
  explaining, proving, finding counterexamples, or critiquing reasoning;
  formal proof is required where the learning outcomes call for it.

- Definitions and notation: Precise meanings, domains, assumptions, and
  conventions.

- Derivation, explanation, or proof: A transparent account of why the
  central result holds.

- Worked examples: From straightforward to subtle, including a
  deliberately contrasted example where useful.

- Common misconceptions: Explain why tempting mistakes fail and how to
  detect them.

- Practice ladder: Recognition, execution, explanation, construction or
  proof, and transfer.

- Boundary cases and connections: Limits, exceptions, counterexamples,
  and links to earlier or later chapters.

- Summary and retrieval prompts: Key relations, questions without notes,
  and cumulative-review pointers.

- Hints and solutions: Sufficient reasoning for independent learning.

- Verification notes: Internal evidence and checking records retained in
  the project repository.

## 5.2 Exercise standards

Exercises should be varied and purposeful. A chapter should generally
include:

- Concept identification and interpretation.

- Routine fluency problems.

- Explanation and error diagnosis.

- Derivation, construction, or proof where appropriate.

- Mixed practice in which the learner chooses the method.

- At least a small number of transfer tasks.

- Representation construction and conversion where appropriate (for
  example, graph from equation, equation from graph, diagram from a
  description, or explanation from a visual).

- Model formulation and independent strategy selection where
  appropriate.

- Proof construction, conjecture testing, counterexample finding, or
  proof critique at the level appropriate to the chapter.

- Cumulative questions that bring back important earlier concepts.

No arbitrary percentage distribution is frozen at this stage; it should
vary by topic. A proof chapter and an introductory arithmetic chapter
require different exercise mixes.

## 5.3 Solution standards

A final answer alone is insufficient for an exercise designed to teach
reasoning. Worked solutions should include the key decisions,
assumptions, transformations, checks, and interpretation. For
challenging questions, staged hints should be available before the full
solution. Deliberate ambiguity, if part of the exercise, must be
explained in the solution.

## 5.4 Memory and review

Use retrieval practice, cumulative review, and spaced revisiting as
practical design tools. Intervals should be adjustable rather than
presented as a universal schedule. The National Research Council’s
proficiency framework supports combining conceptual understanding with
procedural fluency and reasoning \[1\]; it does not dictate one exact
review algorithm. The pilot should measure whether the review features
help actual readers.

## 5.5 Visual asset and figure standards

Each chapter brief must include a visual inventory with a purpose for
each major asset. A visual inventory may include original graphs,
coordinate diagrams, geometric constructions, worked visual proofs,
annotated tables, plots, conceptual maps, or scientific schematics, as
appropriate. There is no quota for decorative images and no assumption
that one visual type fits every subject.

For graph-heavy chapters, at minimum the visuals must support both
reading/interpreting graphs and learning to create or sketch graphs.
Where the topic warrants it, use progressively more demanding examples
and show how the graph relates to its equation, table, context, or
properties. Students should also work in the reverse direction when
mathematically meaningful.

Figure QA must check mathematical correctness, labels, axes, scale,
units, tick marks, domain and range, legends, stated assumptions, visual
contrast, legibility, caption quality, accessibility description,
source/licence status, and consistency with surrounding text. Diagrams
used in proofs should be clearly distinguished from the logical steps
that establish the result. Any chart based on data must be traceable to
its data source or explicitly identified as synthetic/illustrative.

Visual generation is part of production, not a final decoration pass.
The authoring workflow must reserve time for creating, checking,
revising, and visually inspecting all figures. If the necessary visual
cannot be made accurately and readably, the chapter is not ready for
release.

## 5.6 Visualization-building protocol and embedded prompts

Every chapter must consider whether and where readers need to build
representations themselves. Where visual reasoning materially supports
the topic, include several small, in-flow checkpoints near the relevant
explanation; do not rely only on a detached exercise section that readers
may skip. Each checkpoint should have a clear purpose and feedback path.

Select from a progression suited to the concept:

- **Predict before reveal:** Ask what a graph, diagram, shape, trend, or
  transformation should look like before showing the finished visual.
- **Read and annotate:** Point out labels, relationships, invariants,
  axes, important regions, or relevant changes.
- **Complete:** Provide a partial graph, construction, proof diagram, or
  table for the reader to finish.
- **Construct from another form:** Sketch a graph from an equation,
  form an equation from a graph, draw a diagram from a verbal condition,
  or convert data into a suitable plot.
- **Reconstruct:** Hide the worked visual and recreate its essential
  structure or relationships from memory or from the stated conditions.
- **Transform and transfer:** Change a parameter, assumption, scale,
  orientation, or context; predict and explain what must change and what
  remains invariant.

Use support deliberately. A lesson may begin with a complete annotated
example, then remove selected labels or steps, then ask the learner to
construct a new example independently. The aim is not to withhold useful
visuals, nor to ban their use during problem solving. It is to help
readers decide when a visual is useful and to create one when needed.

Each prompt should be followed by an appropriate response route: a
reference answer, an overlay or comparison, a worked construction, a
hint, or a short explanation of acceptable alternatives. Authors should
not equate a neat drawing with a correct argument, or a student's
inability to form a vivid mental image with a lack of mathematical
understanding. Keep accessibility and nonvisual routes in view.

## 5.7 Problem-first design and productive struggle

The authoring brief must state whether problem-first entry is appropriate
for the chapter and why. For a problem-first lesson, record the target
idea, prerequisite knowledge, likely first attempts, common productive
and unproductive strategies, staged hints, the exact point at which the
formal explanation is revealed, and how the explanation will address
the reader's initial reasoning.

A suitable opening challenge should meet five tests:

- It is close enough to readers' current knowledge that they can begin,
  but it exposes a meaningful gap that the new concept will resolve.
- It admits partial progress, more than one plausible approach, or a
  reasoned prediction; it is not merely a trick question.
- It asks learners to generate or compare ideas, not just guess the
  author's hidden answer.
- It provides a reasonable time/space to think and hints or a direct
  route to instruction when needed.
- The following explanation explicitly returns to the challenge and
  consolidates the mathematical idea.

Productive struggle is not the same as unassisted confusion. For
self-study, use a short prompt, an optional graduated hint, and a clearly
signposted reveal. Never require repeated failure before a learner can
see the explanation. Where prior knowledge is insufficient or exploratory
search would be too demanding, begin with a small prerequisite refresher,
a contrasting pair of examples, or a concise worked example, then return
to a fresh attempt. Procedural fluency practice may appropriately use
instruction or worked examples earlier than a conceptual discovery lesson.

Do not describe the project method as experimentally validated. The
chapter pilots should test whether readers engage with the opening task,
understand the later explanation better, and transfer the concept to a
new problem. Revise the sequence if the task mainly creates frustration,
activates irrelevant ideas, or consumes time without helping later
understanding.

# 6. Curriculum architecture

## 6.1 Planned progression

| **Pathway**                 | **Provisional coverage**                                                                                                                                                                      |
|-----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Foundations                 | Quantities, number sense, arithmetic, place value, fractions, ratios, proportion, powers, roots, estimation, and measurement.                                                                 |
| Mathematical language       | Variables, expressions, equations, inequalities, functions, graphs, coordinate geometry, and algebraic transformations.                                                                       |
| Shape and periodicity       | Geometry, trigonometry, spatial reasoning, vectors, and geometric proof.                                                                                                                      |
| Reasoning                   | Logic, sets, quantifiers, proof methods, counterexamples, and induction, introduced gradually and consolidated explicitly.                                                                    |
| University foundations      | Calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, probability, statistics, and mathematical modeling.                                                                                           |
| Applied and advanced routes | Multivariable calculus, differential equations, numerical methods, optimization, real analysis, abstract algebra, complex analysis, topology, measure theory, and selected specialized areas. |

This is not yet a table of contents. The master curriculum map must
decide prerequisites, overlaps, depth, and placement. Some subjects,
such as logic, proof, vectors, and functions, should recur before their
dedicated advanced treatments.

## 6.2 Dependency map rules

Every major topic must have a record of:

- Concept ID and canonical name.

- Learner-facing explanation of the concept.

- Prerequisite concepts.

- Learning outcomes.

- First introduction and later revisits.

- Definitions, theorems, formulas, and methods that belong to it.

- Common misconceptions and known fragile transitions.

- Exercise types and mastery evidence.

- Relevant science applications.

- Sources and verification status.

A concept cannot be placed in a chapter only because it makes the local
story convenient. Its prerequisites must be checked, and necessary
prerequisite gaps must be handled explicitly.

## 6.3 Flexible entry

Readers should be able to use diagnostic questions and prerequisite
links to enter at an appropriate point. A diagnostic must point to
specific concepts needing repair, rather than labeling a learner
globally as “bad at math.”

## 6.4 Progression and review

Important ideas may reappear at increasing levels of abstraction. Each
return should add something: a more general definition, a proof, a new
representation, a broader class of problems, or a new application.
Repetition without additional purpose should be removed.

# 7. Science companion series

## 7.1 Purpose

The companion collection shows how mathematics is used to describe,
infer, predict, and model aspects of the world. It should make
scientific applications more understandable without presenting
mathematical manipulation as a substitute for science.

## 7.2 Initial application domains

- Mathematical language of nature: Units, dimensions, scaling,
  estimation, graphs, measurement, uncertainty, and modeling.

- Physics: Mechanics, energy, momentum, oscillations, electricity,
  magnetism, waves, and later mathematical physics.

- Chemistry: Stoichiometry, concentration, logarithms and pH, kinetics,
  equilibrium, thermodynamics, spectroscopy, and quantum chemistry.

- Living systems: Growth models, probability, genetics, networks,
  epidemiology, statistics, and dynamical models.

- Advanced interdisciplinary modeling: Differential equations, linear
  algebra, probability, optimization, numerical methods, and other
  topics as prerequisite depth permits.

This structure is illustrative. Actual books should be scoped only after
the core dependency map identifies what mathematics learners will have
available.

## 7.3 Application chapter requirements

Each application chapter must identify its mathematical prerequisites;
define variables and units; state assumptions; distinguish idealized
from measured quantities; show how the mathematics enters the model;
interpret the result; examine at least one limitation or sensitivity
where appropriate; and link back to the core book.

## 7.4 Keep categories distinct

Editorial review must distinguish a mathematical theorem from a physical
law, an empirical correlation, a modeling assumption, a numerical
estimate, and an intuitive interpretation. A correct algebraic
derivation cannot by itself establish that a scientific model accurately
describes reality.

## 8.2 Verification lanes

| **Content type**                     | **Required check**                                                                                                                         | **What the check does not prove**                             |
|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Algebraic identity or simplification | Independent symbolic or numerical checks plus a human derivation review                                                                    | That a formula was appropriate for the original problem       |
| Numerical example or answer key      | Independently recompute; automated tests for repeated exercise families                                                                    | That the method or assumptions are correct                    |
| Proof or theorem                     | Check definitions, quantifiers, assumptions, logical steps, counterexamples, and relevant cases; use formal proof checking where justified | That exposition is clear or the theorem is the intended one   |
| Units and physical dimensions        | Dimensional analysis and unit consistency                                                                                                  | That a physical model is empirically valid                    |
| Scientific model                     | Authoritative source check and subject-matter review; verify conditions and limitations                                                    | That every result is universal beyond the model’s domain      |
| Intuition or analogy                 | Seek counterexamples, edge cases, alternate interpretations, and learner feedback                                                          | That the analogy is a proof                                   |
| Historical or factual claim          | Primary or authoritative source where possible; record exact support                                                                       | That the source has been interpreted correctly without review |
| Citation or quotation                | Open the source and verify the exact relevant passage, edition, page or section                                                            | That the source supports more than the verified claim         |
| Learner-facing explanation           | Independent pedagogical critique and pilot testing                                                                                         | That one successful reader represents every audience          |

## 8.3 Mathematical checking toolkit

- Hand derivation: Essential for understanding the logic and
  assumptions.

- Computer algebra: A tool such as SymPy can test symbolic
  transformations and calculations within the tested domain.

- Numerical testing: Substitute carefully chosen values, including
  boundary and edge cases.

- Unit and dimensional checks: Useful for detecting inconsistencies in
  physical formulas.

- Code-based exercise tests: Recompute answers over many generated
  inputs when exercises have reliable programmatic specifications.

- Formal proof checking: Consider Lean and its mathematical ecosystem
  for selected proof-heavy material when the formalization cost is
  justified \[6\].

A tool’s output is evidence only within its scope. A symbolic algebra
system can confirm an equivalence under assumptions but does not
validate the origin of a model. Numerical samples can detect errors but
do not prove a universal identity. A proof assistant checks a formal
statement and proof, so both must match the intended mathematics.

## 8.4 Claim ledger

For every substantial chapter, keep a ledger containing claim ID, exact
claim, type, assumptions, derivation or evidence, source and precise
locator where applicable, verification methods, reviewer, status, date,
and unresolved issues. Claims that are routine and directly derived may
need a lighter record than high-risk scientific or advanced claims, but
every chapter requires a documented review.

## 8.5 AI drafting rules

AI may help draft prose, devise examples, generate candidate exercises,
identify missing cases, suggest source leads, and create automated
checks. It must not be treated as the final verifier. The workflow
should preserve source provenance, use task-specific tests, and keep a
human review route. Evaluation should be repeatable, specific to the
task, and updated as errors are found, consistent with general
evaluation guidance for variable AI outputs \[3\].

The process must not fabricate references, quotations, page numbers,
theorem names, proof results, or claimed review outcomes. If the
evidence cannot be checked, mark the content UNVERIFIED and do not
publish it as established fact.

## 8.6 Independence of review

A second pass by the same model is useful as a challenge exercise, but
it is not independent evidence. High-risk claims should be checked
through genuinely different routes: manual derivation, computational
verification, trusted sources, another qualified reviewer, or
formalization where appropriate.

# 9. Source and copyright policy

## 9.1 Source hierarchy

- Original papers, authoritative standards, official mathematical
  documentation, or primary records for claims that require them.

- Established textbooks and university-published materials for
  explanations, definitions, standard derivations, and curricular
  context.

- Reputable review articles and professional educational resources for
  synthesis.

- General web pages as discovery aids, not as final authority for
  important technical claims.

A source being reputable does not guarantee that every statement in it
applies to our context. Check the relevant passage and scope.

## 9.2 Record exact support

For each sourced claim, preserve title, author or organization, edition
or version, publication date where relevant, stable URL or DOI, exact
page/section/figure where available, access date for changing webpages,
and the claim supported. Distinguish a source used for verification from
one used only to discover another source.

## 9.3 Originality and permissions

Create original explanations, exercises, examples, diagrams, and chapter
structure. Cite sources for facts and intellectual context, but do not
use citation as permission to copy protected prose, illustrations, or
substantial problem sets. Track licences and permissions before reusing
material. Any direct quotation must be necessary, attributed, and
checked against the applicable copyright and permissions policy.

## 9.4 Reference management

Use a persistent bibliography and stable source IDs. Zotero or a
comparable reference manager can store source metadata and PDFs; the
project repository should hold a source-to-claim index so citations
remain meaningful when manuscripts move between tools.

# 10. Review gates and defect severity

A chapter moves through explicit gates. A green status means the defined
checks were completed and recorded; it does not mean that errors are
metaphysically impossible.

## 10.1 Production gates

| **Gate**                        | **Release requirement**                                                                                                      |
|---------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| A — Scope approved              | Learning outcomes, prerequisites, topic boundaries, and sources are identified.                                              |
| B — Draft complete              | All required sections and exercises exist; incomplete sections are visibly marked.                                           |
| C — Mathematical verification   | Derivations, calculations, assumptions, symbols, units, and answer keys checked.                                             |
| D — Pedagogical review          | Intuition, representations, transitions, misconceptions, practice, and independent usability reviewed.                       |
| E — Scientific/reference review | Scientific claims, source support, licenses, and citations checked as applicable.                                            |
| F — Reader test                 | A sample learner can navigate the chapter and complete the targeted tasks; issues are logged.                                |
| G — Editorial freeze            | Corrections are incorporated, cross-references checked, change record updated, and publication files rendered and inspected. |

A chapter should not skip a gate silently. If a gate is not applicable,
record the reason.

## 10.2 Severity levels

| **Severity**  | **Meaning**                                                                                                                           | **Required response**                                                   |
|---------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| S0 — Critical | False theorem, materially wrong scientific claim, dangerous misconception, or error invalidating a major learning outcome.            | Block release; correct and recheck all dependent content.               |
| S1 — Major    | Incorrect derivation, wrong answer key, omitted essential assumption, misleading analogy, or broken prerequisite preventing learning. | Block chapter approval until corrected.                                 |
| S2 — Moderate | Ambiguous wording, incomplete explanation, weak example, missing useful edge case, or inconsistent notation that can mislead.         | Resolve before publication or explicitly document an approved deferral. |
| S3 — Minor    | Typographical, formatting, or stylistic issue that does not change meaning.                                                           | Correct during editorial review; batch safe low-risk edits.             |

Any correction to a mathematical statement, derivation, scientific
assumption, or answer key triggers a regression check of related
examples, exercises, solutions, and cross-references.

## 11.3 Change management

Maintain a decision log for meaningful changes to curriculum, notation,
teaching rules, or scope. When a concept changes, identify dependent
chapters, exercises, science applications, and tests. Do not assume a
local edit has no downstream effect.

## 11.4 Manuscript status labels

Use explicit labels such as OUTLINE, DRAFT, MATH CHECK PENDING, SOURCE
CHECK PENDING, EXTERNAL REVIEW PENDING, PILOT PENDING, APPROVED FOR
LAYOUT, and RELEASED. Never describe a draft as verified, peer-reviewed,
or publication-ready if the corresponding gate has not been completed.

# 12. Roles and decision rights

## 12.1 AI drafting and analysis role

The AI assistant may propose structures, draft text, derive candidate
solutions, generate exercises, run available tools, identify uncertain
claims, organize sources, and revise material from feedback. It must
state uncertainty, retain review records, and never certify its own
unaudited output as error-free.

## 12.2 Project lead role

The project lead controls the vision, scope, priorities, and final
publishing decisions. The project lead need not personally master every
branch of mathematics but must ensure that appropriate external
expertise is obtained before high-risk advanced material is released.

## 12.3 Expert review role

A qualified reviewer checks the mathematics and/or science within their
remit. A reviewer should be told what they are being asked to verify; an
informal glance is not equivalent to a full mathematical or pedagogical
review.

## 12.4 Reader testing role

Pilot readers reveal where the explanation fails in practice. Their
feedback informs revision, but reader preference does not override
mathematical correctness.

## 12.5 Approval rule

AI-generated content alone cannot authorize a chapter for release. Final
release requires the applicable documented checks and the project lead’s
approval. Advanced mathematics and scientific modeling require
specialist review where the potential cost of error is high.

# 13. Pilot plan and acceptance criteria

## 13.1 Pilot selection

- Foundational: Fractions, ratios, and proportional reasoning. This
  tests entry-level clarity, visual representations, arithmetic
  accuracy, prerequisite diagnosis, and independent solution support.

- Advanced application: Electrostatic interaction energy leading into
  the pairwise construction of a dipole interaction. This tests
  mathematical precision, physical assumptions, sign conventions,
  derivation transparency, approximation boundaries, and the ability to
  move between intuitive explanation and formal calculation.

The prototypes need not be full market-ready books. They should be
substantial enough to exercise the chapter template, verification
ledger, exercises, source policy, and reader testing.

## 13.2 Pilot cycle

> **1.** Define the target learner profile and outcomes for each
> prototype.
>
> **2.** Create the concept and prerequisite records.
>
> **3.** Assemble a small, trustworthy reference set.
>
> **4.** Produce the first draft and complete all internal checks.
>
> **5.** Ask at least one appropriate independent reviewer to examine
> high-risk content; use additional reviewers when the topic warrants
> it.
>
> **6.** Test with readers whose prior knowledge fits the intended use.
>
> **7.** Observe whether they can explain, calculate, justify, and
> transfer the target concepts.
>
> **8.** Log defects by severity, revise, and retest the material
> affected.
>
> **9.** Decide whether to refine the method, accept it for wider
> production, or redesign parts of it.

## 13.3 Pilot acceptance criteria

- All stated outcomes map to explanations and assessment tasks.

- Prerequisites and assumptions are explicit.

- All central derivations and worked answers have documented checks.

- No unresolved S0 or S1 defects remain.

- Important sources and quotations have been verified.

- Scientific models and approximations state their scope and
  limitations.

- A reader can use the book without a teacher to understand
  instructions, attempt practice, and check solutions.

- Transfer questions test something not reproduced verbatim in the
  explanation.

- For a chapter using a problem-first opening, the challenge appears
  before the main intuitive explanation and formal instruction; it is
  followed by an achievable initial attempt and clear hints/reveal.

- The later explanation explicitly compares plausible first attempts
  with the formal mathematical account; the opening problem is not a
  decorative hook that the chapter forgets.

- Readers complete at least one independent formulation or
  representation-construction task where the topic supports it.

- Where proof or argument is a learning outcome, readers attempt to
  construct or critique an argument—not merely select or read a supplied
  proof.

- Essential graphs, diagrams, and other visuals have been tested for
  mathematical accuracy, legibility, and usefulness; graph-focused
  chapters include genuine graph interpretation and construction work.

- In-flow visualization prompts have been tested with learners: they
  attempt to predict, annotate, complete, sketch, or reconstruct before
  seeing the full worked visual, then receive clear comparison or feedback.

- The chapter helps learners become more independent with visual
  reasoning without requiring vivid internal imagery or discouraging
  the legitimate use of diagrams as problem-solving tools.

- Learner feedback and known weaknesses are recorded.

- The project lead accepts any remaining S2/S3 issues or records a
  concrete resolution plan before release.

The pilot is a feasibility and quality test, not a controlled
demonstration that the method is superior to conventional books. Any
comparative learning claim would require a suitably designed study with
appropriate measures and sample selection.

# 14. Risks and mitigations

| **Risk**                                        | **Mitigation**                                                                                                       |
|-------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Plausible but false AI-generated explanation    | Claim ledger, independent derivation, computational checks, external review, and explicit uncertainty labels         |
| Citation invented or not actually supportive    | Open every source used for important claims; record exact page/section and claim mapping                             |
| Intuitive analogy becomes a misconception       | Test counterexamples, mark limits, and transition explicitly to formal definitions                                   |
| Curriculum has gaps or jumps                    | Dependency graph, prerequisite checks, and pilot feedback                                                            |
| Exercise answer key contains errors             | Independently recompute, add test cases, and run regression checks after corrections                                 |
| Science model is presented as universal         | Record domain, conditions, approximations, and domain-expert review                                                  |
| Scope becomes too large to manage               | Prioritize a pilot, freeze the core standards, use incremental releases, and avoid premature volume-count promises   |
| Chapters become inconsistent                    | Shared notation guide, term list, canonical concept IDs, and change log                                              |
| Reader appreciates the style but does not learn | Assess explanations, independent problem-solving, transfer, and delayed retrieval—not satisfaction alone             |
| Copyright or attribution problem                | Write original material, check licence terms, maintain permissions records, and review borrowed assets               |
| Context lost between writing sessions           | Maintain the Project Bible, curriculum repository, chapter status, source records, and decision log outside the chat |

## Appendix C — Chapter release checklist

- Learning outcomes are observable and assessed.

- Prerequisites and entry points are explicit.

- The chapter's entry sequence is intentional: where problem-first is
  appropriate, a concrete challenge and brief first attempt precede the
  main intuition/explanation; any exception is justified in the chapter
  brief.

- All symbols and terms are defined before reliance on them.

- Each major intuitive interpretation is compatible with the formal
  mathematics.

- Analogy boundaries and counterexamples are supplied when needed.

- Derivations have no unexplained or invalid transitions.

- Assumptions, domains, units, sign conventions, and approximations are
  checked where applicable.

- Worked examples and every published answer key were independently
  checked.

- Exercises cover explanation, procedure, mixed method selection,
  reasoning, and transfer where appropriate.

- Sources are real, opened, correctly represented, and linked to the
  claims they support.

- Copyright and licence checks are complete for reused material.

- Scientific content has appropriate domain review.

- No unresolved S0 or S1 defects remain.

- Relevant regression checks have passed after the final edit.

- Reader testing is complete or its absence is explicitly disclosed.

- Final layout, equations, figures, tables, graphs, captions, in-flow prompts, and page
  breaks have been visually inspected.

- Each major visual has a defined learning purpose and matches the
  mathematics in the prose and equations.

- Graph-heavy chapters contain accurate graphs and tasks in both graph
  interpretation and graph construction/sketching, where appropriate.

- Relevant explanations contain short in-flow visualization prompts;
  visualization is not left only to end-of-chapter exercises.

- The support/fading plan is visible: learners interpret supplied visuals,
  practise partial or prompted construction, and attempt independent
  representation-building or transfer where appropriate.

- Feedback or reference solutions explain how to compare a learner-made
  representation with a mathematically valid version. Accessible
  alternatives are available; vivid mental imagery is not required.

- Axes, scales, units, labels, legends, domains/ranges, captions, and
  accessible descriptions are present wherever relevant.

- Exercise sets include independent equation/model construction and
  proof/conjecture/counterexample tasks when appropriate to the topic.

- Mathematical formulation, interpretation, and mathematical
  transformation/derivation are assessed in a balanced way.

- Version, status, reviewers, and unresolved lower-severity issues are
  recorded.
